1. The Security Mindset — running the reveal

s7Two columns. The right-hand column is hidden until you press the button.

This is the slide that sets up everything that follows. The point is not that developers are careless. It is that the sentences which quietly become security controls are always reasonable sentences — which is exactly why nobody challenges them. If the room accepts this, every demo afterwards lands as "oh, that's the pattern again" rather than "wow, bad code".

How to run it — roughly three minutes

  1. Do not press the button yet. Read three of the left-hand sentences out loud, slowly. Pick the ones closest to your audience — for a bank, "it is an internal API" and "we are on HTTPS" usually land hardest.
  2. Ask for agreement. "Has anyone here said something like this, or heard it in a design review this month?" Hands go up. That is the moment you need.
  3. Let them own it. "Good — because none of these are wrong. Every one of them is a competent engineer making a defensible statement."
  4. Now press the button. Then stop talking for about five seconds while they read.
  5. Deliver the closing line yourself, don't let the slide carry it alone.

"Every sentence on the left is true. The problem is that each one is doing the job of a security control without actually being one. Nobody decided that 'nobody knows the URL' was our access control — it just quietly became it."

Why this version is written for you rather than for engineers

The earlier draft of this slide compared "what the builder assumes" with "what the attacker sees", using examples like decompiling a mobile app. That is accurate but it is a developer's list — a senior leader cannot act on it. This version uses sentences you will actually hear in a meeting, which turns the slide into something you can use on a Tuesday afternoon: when you hear one of these, ask what the real control is.

"Isn't this just saying our engineers are wrong?" — No, and say so firmly. Every statement is factually correct. The failure is that a true statement was allowed to substitute for a control. That is a process and review gap, not a competence gap. Getting this right protects your relationship with the engineering leaders in the room.

2. The Threat Economy

s8"An exploit is not the whole attack. It is one product in a supply chain."

Leaders carry a mental image of a lone attacker who picked them. Almost everything about that image leads to bad budget decisions. This slide replaces it with the correct one: a specialised, commercial supply chain in which different people do different jobs and sell to each other.

The four roles on the slide, in plain terms

RoleWhat they actually do
Access brokerSpecialises in getting a foothold — valid credentials, an exposed remote-access service, a compromised supplier. They usually do not attack you further. They sell the way in to somebody who will.
Exploit / toolingBuilds and maintains the capability. Increasingly sold as a product with versioning, documentation and support, because the buyers are not necessarily skilled.
OperatorBuys access, moves through the network, finds what matters, steals or encrypts it. This is the group that behaves like an intruder in your environment.
MonetisationExtortion negotiation, fraud, or resale of the data. A separate skill set again — often a separate group entirely.

The three points worth making out loud

  • Specialisation means scale. Nobody in this chain needs to be good at all four things. That is precisely why the volume of attacks keeps rising — the hard parts have been productised.
  • You are usually found, not chosen. Most initial access is opportunistic: the internet is scanned continuously, and whatever answers gets tried. "Why would anyone target us?" is the wrong question. Nobody targeted you; you simply answered.
  • You do not have to be unbreakable. You have to be more expensive than the next organisation on the list. Every control that adds cost and time shifts the economics, and the economics are what decide whether anyone finishes the job.

"There is no single villain here. There is a market. Somebody sells the way in, somebody else buys it, and a third party handles the money. When we talk about raising the cost of an attack, this is the chain we are raising it on — and we only have to break it in one place."

Connecting it to the leadership lens on the slide

The card at the bottom lists secure engineering, identity controls, segmentation, detection and rehearsed recovery. Make the mapping explicit, because this is the slide's practical payoff:

  • Secure engineering and identity make the access broker's job harder — the first link.
  • Segmentation makes the operator's job slower — the middle link.
  • Detection shortens how long they have.
  • Rehearsed recovery destroys the monetisation step: an organisation that can restore does not need to negotiate.

"So should we just buy cyber insurance?" — Insurance covers some of the financial loss. It does not restore your systems, your customers' trust, or your regulatory position, and insurers increasingly require evidence of exactly these controls before they will pay. It is a financial instrument, not a control.

"Are we actually a target?" — Use the point above: you are found, not chosen. Then make it concrete: anything of yours reachable from the internet is being probed today, by automation, continuously.

3. When Cyber Risk Becomes Business Survival

s9Three cases: Code Spaces, NotPetya / Maersk, Travelex.

This is the only slide in the deck that argues cyber risk can be existential rather than expensive. Use it sparingly and precisely — overclaiming here is the fastest way to lose a sceptical CFO, and you will not get their attention back.

Code Spaces — the short, brutal one

A code-hosting business. An attacker gained control of its cloud administration console and, when challenged, destroyed the environment — including the backups, which lived in the same account under the same credentials. The company could not rebuild and ceased operating.

The lesson to draw: it was not the intrusion that ended the company, it was that a single identity could reach both production and the backups. That is a design decision, and it is one your own teams have made somewhere.

NotPetya and Maersk — the one to spend the most time on

Destructive malware spread through a trusted software update channel and propagated across global networks within minutes. Maersk, which was not the intended target, lost the great majority of its end-user devices and servers and ran a global shipping operation on manual processes for roughly ten days while rebuilding.

The detail worth telling: the rebuild depended on recovering a domain controller from an office in Ghana that happened to be offline during the attack because of a local power cut. Their recovery rested on an accident.

"Their disaster recovery plan worked because of a power cut in Ghana. I would like us to be able to answer this question without relying on luck: if we lost our identity infrastructure tonight, what is our actual path back, and has anyone ever walked it?"

Travelex — handle with care

A ransomware incident over the New Year period took customer-facing services offline for an extended time, and the business later entered administration during a period that also included severe pandemic disruption to foreign exchange.

Do not say cyber killed Travelex. Say the incident "contributed to" or "compounded" an already difficult position. If you overstate it, someone in the room who knows the detail will correct you, and the whole slide loses its force. The same caution applies to any breach-cost figure you quote — if you cannot name where the number came from, round it or drop it.

The single idea this slide must leave behind

Across all three, the difference between an expensive incident and a terminal one was recovery, not prevention. Everyone in the room already funds prevention. Far fewer have ever tested a full restore of the systems the business actually runs on.

"We have backups." — The right follow-up is: are they reachable with the same credentials as production, when were they last restored end-to-end rather than spot-checked, and how long did that restore take? Code Spaces had backups too.

4. AI in Security

Phase 5Seven slides: AI as attacker, the lowered bar, AI as defender, both sides equally, what AI still cannot do, and the closing force-multiplier line. Budget about 20 minutes.

Your audience arrives with one of two beliefs: AI has made attackers unstoppable, or AI is vendor noise. Both are wrong, and the section works only if you refuse both. The honest position is narrower and more useful: AI changes speed, scale and accessibility. It has not changed the underlying vulnerabilities. Every flaw demonstrated in this session predates modern AI by decades.

On the offensive side — what is genuinely different

  • The entry barrier has dropped. Work that needed real expertise can now be attempted by someone who can describe what they want. Expect more attempts, from more people.
  • Iteration is faster. The loop of trying something, failing, and adjusting is now very cheap. Defences that relied on attackers giving up early age badly.
  • Social engineering got better, not just faster. This is the change your staff will actually meet. The old advice — look for clumsy language — no longer works, and synthetic voice makes "I recognised their voice" an unsafe basis for approving anything.

Resist the temptation to claim AI is "writing novel attacks". It mostly reproduces and adapts known techniques very quickly. Successful intrusion still needs access, context, persistence and operational judgement. If you overstate this, the technical people in the room will discount the whole section — and they are the ones you need on side.

On the defensive side

  • Review at a scale humans never had. Every commit can be examined, not just the ones somebody had time for.
  • Triage. The practical win for most security operations teams is not detection but sorting — cutting the queue so analysts spend attention where it matters.
  • Explanation. Turning a technical finding into something an engineering team will act on has always been a bottleneck. This is a real and unglamorous gain.

The three limits to state plainly

  1. Business context. A model can tell you an authorisation check is missing. It cannot tell you whether that page is deliberately public. Only your people know that.
  2. Risk acceptance. Deciding to carry a risk for ninety days with compensating controls is an accountable human judgement. No model owns it, and no regulator will accept that one did.
  3. Validating its own output. It will produce confident, plausible, wrong security advice. Anything acted on needs a competent human who could have reached the answer themselves.

"AI has not changed what is wrong with our applications. Every vulnerability you broke today is twenty years old. What has changed is how many people can attempt them, how fast, and how convincing the social engineering around them has become. The same leverage is available to our defenders — and the only question that matters is which side uses it consistently."

"Will AI replace our security team?" — No, and the closing slide makes the argument for you: AI can find the flaw in milliseconds, but a human decided whether to fund the review that would have caught it, and a human has to answer at two in the morning. It changes what the team spends its time on, not whether you need one.

"Should we block AI tools internally?" — Blocking mostly moves usage somewhere you cannot see. The more productive questions are what data may go into which tools, who reviews output before it ships, and whether your people have an approved option that is good enough that they do not route around it.

"Are attackers using this against us today?" — Yes, most visibly in social engineering quality. That is the part worth briefing your workforce on this quarter.

Landing the section

Close on the force-multiplier slide and resist adding to it. The sentence you want them repeating afterwards is that AI lowered the bar for attackers and raised the ceiling for defenders — and that which of those matters more to your organisation is a decision, not a forecast.
